Explainable machine learning for two-phase heat transfer

“A Diverse and Explainable Machine Learning Framework for Multi-property Prediction for R32 in Helical Pipe Evaporators”
Ashfaq Mahmud Khan, Dipayan Mondal, Saphin Hasan, Tasnin Hasan Anto, Oliur Rahman · manuscript in preparation

Boiling simulations are accurate but slow. I turned 149 validated CFD simulations of R32 boiling in helical coils into fast surrogate models, compared seven machine learning methods, and then opened the best models with Shapley values, partial dependence and individual conditional expectation plots to check that they learned real physics.

The framework
From physics to an explainable design tool

Four design inputs go into a validated two-phase CFD model. Seven machine learning models learn the mapping, and explainability tools turn the best models back into physical insight.

4 inputs

  • Pipe diameter 5–10 mm
  • Coil diameter 10–60 mm
  • Mass flux 300–1178 kg/m²s
  • Wall temperature 300–400 K

CFD engine

  • Volume of fluid 3D
  • Lee phase change R32
  • Realizable k-ε turbulence
  • Validated ≤ 7.4% error

7 ML models

  • XGBoost, random forest trees
  • LightGBM, decision tree trees
  • Gaussian process kernel
  • k-NN, neural network other

Explain

  • Shapley values global + local
  • Interaction values pairs
  • Partial dependence 1D, 2D
  • ICE curves per case

What matters. Global Shapley importance: mass flux dominates, then coil diameter.
Inside the model. Shapley beeswarm and decision plots for heat transfer.
Heat transfer is nearly additive. The strongest pair, coil diameter × mass flux (56.9 W/m²K), is only about 11% of the mass-flux main effect.
Pressure drop is strongly coupled. Pipe × coil diameter alone carries 52% of all pairwise interaction (1,046 Pa), so the two diameters must be chosen together.
Why these pairs? In the Dean number De = Re (d/D)1/2 with Re = Gd/μ, mass flux, pipe diameter and coil diameter multiply each other. A multiplicative structure cannot be additive, so the model had to learn exactly these interactions.

−87%Pipe diameter acts as a gate. Partial dependence falls from about 32,400 Pa at 5 mm to 4,300 Pa above 9 mm. Below about 7 mm every other variable is amplified; above it the response is small and robust.

Design takeaways
What a designer can use tomorrow
01

Two levers for heat transfer

Mass flux and coil diameter explain 84.4% of the HTC attribution. Tune these first.

02

Tight coil, high flow

Small coils with high mass flux give the highest HTC, and there a larger pipe helps rather than hurts.

03

Stay above 7 mm

Pipes of about 7 mm or more give a low, robust pressure drop that is insensitive to coil size, flux and temperature.

04

Wall temperature is a minor knob

Within 300–400 K it shifts HTC by only about 135 W/m²K and contributes under 4% of either output.

Small test set30 unseen cases, so the ranking of the top models is confirmed with cross-validation.
Sparse high-ΔP tailFew cases above 20,000 Pa; more CFD runs there would strengthen the surrogate.
Next stepPhysics-informed models and accumulated local effects to stay reliable outside the training range.
