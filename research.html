PUBLISHED · FIRST AUTHOR

Flow boiling of R32 in helical pipe evaporators

Khan, Mondal, Alom, Ghosh. International Communications in Heat and Mass Transfer (2026). Read paper ↗

❓The question

R32 is replacing high global-warming refrigerants, and helical coils pack large heat transfer areas into compact evaporators. But boiling inside a coil is strongly three-dimensional: where does heat transfer peak, and what does the coil cost in pressure drop?

🔧My approach

I built three-dimensional volume of fluid simulations with the Lee phase-change model and a realizable k-epsilon turbulence model, validated them against published experiments, and ran 73 Latin hypercube cases across mass flux, wall temperature and coil geometry.

💡What I found

Heat transfer peaks at the first helical turn, where curvature-driven Dean vortices are strongest, and the coil outperforms straight and U-bend designs at a measurable pressure-drop cost.

Key findings
  • Heat transfer peaks at the first helical turn, where Dean vortices are strongest.
  • A new Nusselt correlation built from Reynolds, Dean and Jakob numbers captures the simulations with R² = 0.987.
  • The helical coil beats straight and U-bend evaporators on heat transfer, with a clear pressure-drop trade-off.
Open question I want to pursue: at high mass flux the Lee model limited vaporization to 18.7 to 39.8% of the theoretical value. Better phase-change closures are exactly what I hope to work on in a PhD.

PUBLISHED · NEXT ENERGY

Condensation of R32 in inclined smooth tubes

Islam, Mondal, Khan. Next Energy 14, 101168 (2027). Read paper ↗

❓The question

Condenser tubes are rarely perfectly horizontal. At intermediate angles, gravity and interfacial shear compete to shape the liquid film. When does tube orientation change heat transfer, and when can designers ignore it?

🔧My approach

I simulated R32 condensation in a 5 mm tube at eleven inclinations, from vertical upward to vertical downward, at two mass fluxes. The model uses the volume of fluid method, SST k-omega turbulence and phase-change source terms written as a compiled Fluent user-defined function.

💡What I found

Inclination matters most at low vapour quality and mass flux, where gravity redistributes the film. Above a modified Froude number of about 20 the flow becomes shear-dominated and orientation effects fade.

5 mm tube → Hexahedral mesh → Volume of fluid → Compiled user-defined function → 11 angles × 2 mass fluxes → Froude regime analysis

Key findings
  • Gravity-driven redistribution of the liquid film explains the trend: thicker films add thermal resistance.
  • Higher mass flux increases interfacial shear and weakens orientation sensitivity.
  • Steep downward flow can produce pressure recovery at low vapour quality.
Next step: experimental validation and a wider range of tube diameters and refrigerants.

PUBLISHED · THERM. SCI. ENG. PROG.

Liquid cooling of lithium-ion battery modules

Niloy, Mondal, Khan. Thermal Science and Engineering Progress 75, 104774 (2026). Read paper ↗

❓The question

Uneven cell temperatures shorten battery life. A cooling channel can even them out, but every gain costs pumping power. How do we find the best balance without simulating every design?

🔧My approach

For a twenty-cell array of 18650 cells, I helped build transient conjugate heat transfer simulations of a cross-flow liquid cooling channel, trained a second-order response-surface surrogate on 42 cases, and optimized two objectives at once.

💡What I found

The knee of the Pareto front gives a balanced design that cuts cell-to-cell temperature non-uniformity by 69.7% while keeping pressure drop in check.

  • Response surfaces map temperature spread and pressure drop over channel width and inlet velocity.

  • A knee-point rule selects the design without arbitrary weighting factors.

FEATURED · FIRST AUTHOR · IN PREPARATION

Explainable machine learning for two-phase heat transfer

Khan et al. Manuscript in preparation.

❓The question

Two-phase simulations are accurate but slow. Can a machine learning model predict heat transfer and pressure drop instantly, and can we check that it learned real physics rather than noise?

🔧My approach

Using 149 validated simulations of R32 helical evaporators, I trained and compared seven models: random forest, XGBoost, decision tree, k-nearest neighbours, neural network, Gaussian process and LightGBM. I then opened the best ones with Shapley values, partial dependence and individual conditional expectation plots.

💡What I found

XGBoost predicts heat transfer best and random forest predicts pressure drop best. Shapley analysis shows mass flux dominates, followed by coil diameter, matching the physics from the simulations.

Flow-driven corrosion in carbon steel pipelines

Das, Mondal, Khan. Results in Surfaces and Interfaces 24, 100851 (2026). Read paper ↗

❓The question

Pipelines corrode fastest where flow and chemistry combine. Where along a bent pipe does sulfate-induced corrosion concentrate, and how do velocity and temperature change it?

🔧My approach

We coupled turbulent flow with Butler-Volmer electrochemistry and species transport in a bent carbon steel pipe, then varied velocity, temperature and flow regime and compared the predictions with literature models.

💡What I found

Wall shear stress peaks at the bend, where corrosion risk concentrates, and higher temperature raises corrosion rate by about 30%.

Bio-based composites for thermal wall insulation

Sharikul, Mondal, Nath, Khan, Islam, Hasib. Journal of Building Engineering, 134, 117743 (2026). doi:10.1016/j.jobe.2026.117743

❓The question

Water hyacinth is an invasive weed that clogs waterways in Bangladesh. Can it become a useful filler that makes natural-fibre composites better at keeping heat out of buildings?

🔧My approach

We turned water hyacinth into powder and added it at 0 to 3 wt% to jute and juco fibre epoxy composites. We measured mechanical strength, microstructure, water absorption and thermal conductivity, then modelled heat flow through an insulated interior wall for summer and winter.

💡What I found

Adding the filler lowers thermal conductivity to about 0.037 W/m·K, up to 24% below the unfilled composite, and reduces heat transfer through the wall in both seasons.

UNDER REVIEW · THIN-WALLED STRUCTURES

3D-printed PETG Omega structures for energy absorption

Rahman, Khan, Farabi. Thin-Walled Structures (under review).

❓The question

Protective structures must crush progressively and soak up energy while staying light. How does the curvature of an Omega-shaped member decide the way a 3D-printed polymer lattice collapses, and how much energy it absorbs?

🔧My approach

We designed three architectures by changing only the central angle of the curved member (Ω-130, Ω-180, Ω-260), printed them in PETG by fused filament fabrication, characterised the material in tension, crushed the structures quasi-statically and built a 3D finite element model in Abaqus to reproduce the collapse.

💡What I found

Curvature sets the collapse mode. The semicircular Ω-180 balances axial and bending load transfer and collapses layer by layer, giving the best stiffness, strength and specific energy absorption, about 85% higher than Ω-130.

Key findings
  • Three collapse modes: whole-height flattening (Ω-130), layer-by-layer collapse (Ω-180) and delayed localised collapse (Ω-260).
  • Ω-180 reached a compressive modulus of 8.95 MPa, peak stress of 4.80 MPa and toughness of 1.12 MJ/m³.
  • The FE model matched modulus within 5.2%, peak stress within 3.3% and SEA within 2.0%.
